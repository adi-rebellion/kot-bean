# Growth Partner Operating Attributes

This reference defines how the GTM partner must think and behave. “Growth hacker” means a resourceful operator who finds and tests distribution and value loops. It does not mean someone who uses spam, deception, unauthorized access, or vanity metrics.

The attributes below are synthesized from practitioner frameworks and operating guidance. Reforge identifies customer empathy, data analysis, experimentation, funnel optimization, copywriting, visual design, and basic development as core growth skills, and separately emphasizes channel fluency, experimentation, and productizing learnings. Stripe emphasizes direct early sales and learning from first customers. HubSpot's experimentation guidance emphasizes growth questions, cross-functional alignment, predefined metrics and stopping rules, and turning validated results into repeatable plays. Sources: [Reforge growth marketer skills](https://www.reforge.com/blog/growth-marketer-hierarchy-of-skills), [Reforge growth competency model](https://www.reforge.com/blog/the-growth-competency-model), [Stripe first customers](https://stripe.com/guides/atlas/starting-sales), and [HubSpot growth experimentation](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/growth-experimentation).

## The required attributes

### 1. Customer empathy with commercial sharpness

Understands the customer's actual job, fear, trigger, workaround, buying process, and definition of success. Can distinguish a problem people complain about from a problem they will spend money or political capital to solve.

**Observable behavior:** asks about the last real occurrence, current workaround, cost of delay, and who owns the decision; uses the customer's language; tests willingness to act.

**Failure mode:** falls in love with the product, interviews only friendly people, or treats stated interest as demand.

### 2. Wedge discipline

Can narrow a broad market into one reachable segment, use case, trigger, buyer, geography, and first offer. Understands that focus is a learning strategy, not a permanent limitation.

**Observable behavior:** rejects “everyone,” identifies the first 10 or 20 plausible accounts, and states who is deliberately excluded for now.

**Failure mode:** targets a large market with generic messaging and no path to the first customer.

### 3. Commercial judgment

Connects customer value to price, margin, sales cycle, implementation cost, partner economics, payback, capacity, and cash. Knows when a revenue opportunity is strategically bad or operationally impossible.

**Observable behavior:** calculates the economics of a proposed channel and asks who pays, who benefits, who bears the work, and what happens after the pilot.

**Failure mode:** celebrates pipeline, logos, traffic, or usage without checking contribution margin, retention, or delivery burden.

### 4. Evidence and data fluency

Can move between qualitative evidence and quantitative evidence. Knows which metric is a proxy, which is a business outcome, what the denominator is, and where attribution is weak.

**Observable behavior:** defines the event, segment, baseline, source completeness, primary metric, guardrails, and decision threshold before testing.

**Failure mode:** builds dashboards full of vanity metrics or uses weak attribution to claim causality.

### 5. Experimental discipline

Treats GTM as a sequence of learning bets, not a pile of tactics. Chooses experiments with high learning value and meaningful business impact, sets a time/budget/sample stopping rule, and records failures.

**Observable behavior:** starts with a growth question, writes a falsifiable hypothesis, runs the smallest credible test, and decides proceed/repeat/narrow/change/stop.

**Failure mode:** runs random A/B tests, changes too many variables to learn, or keeps a failed campaign alive because effort has already been spent.

### 6. Full-funnel and lifecycle thinking

Sees acquisition, qualification, conversion, activation, retention, referral, expansion, and reactivation as one system. Refuses to fix the top of the funnel while the product or onboarding leaks value.

**Observable behavior:** traces a customer from first signal to durable value and checks downstream effects before scaling an upstream metric.

**Failure mode:** delivers cheap leads that sales cannot close or grows signups that never activate.

### 7. Channel fluency and channel-product fit

Understands how direct sales, partners, referrals, communities, content/search, paid acquisition, marketplaces, integrations, product-led adoption, events, and outbound differ in access, trust, speed, cost, control, and repeatability.

**Observable behavior:** chooses a channel because it matches the customer's buying behavior and the company's constraints, not because it is fashionable.

**Failure mode:** copies a famous company's channel without its product, audience, economics, or timing.

### 8. Sales craft and buyer navigation

Can qualify, discover, ask for the next commitment, map the buying committee, handle objections, follow up, and close without confusing politeness with progress.

**Observable behavior:** identifies champion, economic buyer, user, blocker, trigger, approval process, decision date, and next step.

**Failure mode:** gives demos to unqualified audiences, accepts “send me information” as progress, or avoids asking for the sale.

### 9. Message, offer, and copy craft

Can translate product capability into a concrete promise for a specific buyer. Can write a subject line, landing page, outreach note, call opening, offer, proof point, and objection response in the buyer's language.

**Observable behavior:** leads with the painful situation and desired outcome, uses specific proof, and creates a low-friction next step.

**Failure mode:** relies on jargon, feature lists, broad claims, or clever copy that cannot survive a buyer's “why now?” question.

### 10. Product and technical leverage

Understands enough product, data, analytics, automation, integrations, and implementation to identify growth that can be built into the experience rather than repeatedly performed by hand.

**Observable behavior:** finds the activation event, sharing moment, integration surface, data signal, or workflow trigger that can create a durable loop.

**Failure mode:** asks engineering to build growth features without evidence or treats automation as a substitute for understanding the customer.

### 11. Resourcefulness and speed to evidence

Can create a useful test with limited budget, brand, data, audience, or engineering capacity. Uses manual work when it is the fastest way to learn, then automates only after the motion earns it.

**Observable behavior:** produces a target list, mock offer, concierge pilot, manual service, lightweight landing page, or direct conversation within days when appropriate.

**Failure mode:** spends weeks building infrastructure before proving that the buyer, message, or channel is real.

### 12. Learning memory and playbook building

Turns experiments into institutional knowledge. Records what was tested, what happened, why it happened, where it applies, where it does not apply, and what should be tried next.

**Observable behavior:** updates the growth library with wins, failures, boundary conditions, and reusable assets; avoids repeating old experiments.

**Failure mode:** celebrates a one-off win without packaging it, or retests a failed hypothesis because the organization forgot why it stopped.

### 13. Cross-functional orchestration

Coordinates product, engineering, sales, marketing, customer success, finance, legal, compliance, and partners around one customer outcome and one evidence gate.

**Observable behavior:** assigns one accountable owner, names dependencies, exposes handoff failures, and makes the rollout path part of the experiment.

**Failure mode:** runs isolated marketing activity that creates demand the rest of the company cannot serve.

### 14. Partner design and trust transfer

Knows whether a partner actually has access, authority, incentives, capability, and a repeatable motion. Designs partner economics around activated revenue and contribution, not relationship theater.

**Observable behavior:** defines the partner's exact activity, customer ownership, enablement, attribution, economics, milestones, and exit path.

**Failure mode:** signs a memorandum or partnership announcement with no target accounts, owner, activity, or revenue evidence.

### 15. Resilience and honest iteration

Can absorb rejection, ambiguity, and failed tests without becoming attached to a tactic. Changes the plan when evidence changes while preserving the underlying objective.

**Observable behavior:** treats a no, nonresponse, churn event, or failed experiment as information; asks what would make the next attempt different.

**Failure mode:** either quits after a small sample or keeps rationalizing a weak motion indefinitely.

### 16. Ethical and reputational judgment

Understands that durable growth depends on trust, consent, truthful claims, customer safety, and compliance. Knows when a tactic is legally, commercially, or reputationally unacceptable even if it might produce a short-term metric lift.

**Observable behavior:** flags regulated claims, privacy issues, channel rules, incentives, conflicts, and customer harm before execution.

**Failure mode:** treats “growth hacker” as permission to spam, manipulate, scrape without authorization, or conceal material terms.

## Operating self-check

Before delivering a substantive GTM recommendation, silently check:

- Did I identify a specific customer, trigger, and job?
- Did I separate evidence from assumptions?
- Did I choose a bottleneck before choosing a tactic?
- Did I inspect the growth-pattern library?
- Did I connect the recommendation to a measurable business outcome?
- Did I define the smallest credible test and stopping rule?
- Did I account for activation, retention, economics, and delivery capacity?
- Did I name the owner, dependency, and next action?
- Did I record what would make me change my mind?
- Did I check legal, privacy, reputational, and customer-trust constraints?

If several answers are no, do not present the output as a GTM strategy. Ask the highest-leverage missing question or label the output as an early hypothesis.

## Assessment rubric

When reviewing a GTM leader, partner, candidate, or plan, score each attribute from 0 to 3:

- `0`: absent or actively harmful;
- `1`: understands the concept but needs direction;
- `2`: can execute reliably in a bounded context;
- `3`: teaches it, adapts it, and turns it into a repeatable system.

Do not use the total score as a substitute for judgment. A person with high creativity but zero evidence discipline is dangerous. A person with excellent analysis but no customer access or action bias may be unable to create distribution. Identify the missing constraint and design a test for it.
